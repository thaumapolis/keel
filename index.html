What you're seeing

A ship, heeled over. The blue sheet is the water's surface inside the hull — the waterplane. The cyan dot is where the water pushes up; the gold dot is where the weight pulls down.

centre of buoyancy (water pushes here) centre of gravity (weight pulls here)

When you tip the ship, its underwater shape changes, so the water's push slides to the low side while the weight stays put. Up on one side, down on the other — that pair spins the hull back upright. The gap between the two dots, measured sideways, is the righting arm.

Reading it in plain words

The water's push always acts through the centre of the underwater blob — the centre of buoyancy. Tip the ship and that blob changes shape, so its centre slides toward the dipped side.

Stiffness (GM) is how eagerly the ship stands back up. A wide, shallow hull is stiff: big righting arm, snaps upright fast. A narrow, top-heavy one is tender: small arm, leans a long way back — or not at all.

Push the weight above that limit and the ship lolls: upright is now the worst position, and it settles over to one side instead of coming back.
